## Addressable Market

### The HVAC universe

The North American HVAC distribution market was roughly $66B in 2024, growing at ~6% CAGR. The broader HVAC equipment and services market in the US is $76B+ and the entire HVAC industry globally exceeds $350B. These are the numbers market research firms publish, but they're not your addressable market — they're the ocean your customers swim in.

### Your actual addressable market

You're not selling HVAC equipment. You're selling operational intelligence software to a specific subset of HVAC businesses. The addressable market calculation works like this:

**Tier 1 — PE-backed HVAC distributors (Phase 1 target)**
There are over 10,000 small-to-medium HVAC distributors in the US. The mid-market segment (companies doing $50M-$500M) represents roughly 25% of the industry. PE-backed platforms within that segment are your immediate target. Based on the deal data, there are likely 30-60 PE-backed HVAC distribution platforms in the US right now, with new ones forming every quarter as PE firms launch new platform builds.

At your pricing ($150k-$250k initial + $8k-$15k/month ongoing), each platform customer is worth roughly $250k-$430k in year-one revenue and $100k-$180k in recurring revenue per year after that. Each bolt-on acquisition is an upsell worth $20k-$40k.

Total Tier 1 TAM: 30-60 platforms × $300k average year-one revenue = $9M-$18M in year-one addressable revenue. Recurring base after year one: $3M-$11M/year.

This is small. It's enough to build a real business, but not enough to sustain a venture-scale company alone.

**Tier 2 — PE-backed HVAC service rollups (Phase 2 target)**
There are 269 PE-backed HVAC service platform companies (not counting add-ons) and 667 total PE-backed HVAC companies including add-ons, per industry data. These are larger platforms on average than the distributors, and your price point is higher ($200k-$350k initial + $15k-$25k/month ongoing).

Total Tier 2 TAM: conservatively 100-200 platforms that fit your profile × $400k average year-one revenue = $40M-$80M in year-one addressable revenue. Recurring base: $18M-$60M/year.

**Tier 3 — Adjacent trades (plumbing, electrical, other industrial)**
Plumbing and electrical distribution and service rollups follow the same PE-driven pattern, just slightly behind HVAC in consolidation maturity. The same architecture and similar ontology templates extend to these markets. Per WSJ, PE purchased nearly 800 companies across HVAC, plumbing, and electrical since 2022, and the total market of private companies "ripe for acquisition" across HVAC alone is 29,000+.

Tier 3 adds 2-3x the addressable market of Tiers 1+2, but requires template adaptation and new domain expertise per trade.

**Tier 4 — Non-PE mid-market companies (the long tail)**
Thousands of non-PE-owned mid-market HVAC distributors and contractors who have the same data mess but less urgency and less budget. Lower price point, longer sales cycle, but massive volume. This is the SaaS play: productize the pipeline so these companies can self-onboard at $2k-$5k/month without FDE support.

**Total addressable market across all tiers:**
- Near-term (Tiers 1+2, PE-backed HVAC only): $50M-$100M in year-one revenue potential, $20M-$70M in steady-state recurring
- Medium-term (add Tier 3, adjacent trades): $150M-$300M
- Long-term (add Tier 4, self-serve product): $500M+

These numbers are rough but directionally correct and defensible to a YC partner or investor. The key story: Tier 1 alone is small but enough to bootstrap. Tiers 1+2 are enough for a real venture-scale company. Tiers 3+4 are the $100M+ ARR path that makes this a big outcome.

## Revenue Scenarios

All three scenarios assume you start building in month 1, first customer closes in month 3, and the pricing model from the strategy doc (initial deployment fee + monthly recurring + upsell per acquisition).

**What went wrong in this scenario:** You didn't find a co-founder, so you're doing everything yourself. Sales cycles were 4-6 months instead of 2-3 because PE operating partners wanted to see more references before committing. Pricing got negotiated down. One customer churned. You never applied to YC.

## Exit Scenarios

Exit timing and valuation depend heavily on which revenue scenario plays out and what kind of buyer you attract. There are three exit paths.

### Path 1: Acqui-hire / small acquisition (Bearish outcome)

If you're in the bearish scenario by year 3-4 ($2-3M revenue, small team, niche product), the most likely exit is an acquisition by one of the HVAC-adjacent software companies — ServiceTitan, BuildOps, DDI System, or possibly a larger distributor like Watsco. They'd be buying the product, the customer relationships, and the team. Not a life-changing outcome but not nothing.

**Likely timing:** Year 3-5
**Likely valuation:** $3M-$10M (1-3x revenue, acqui-hire premium)
**Who buys you:** HVAC software vendor, a large distributor, or a PE firm that wants the tool in-house for its portfolio

### Path 2: Strategic acquisition (Base outcome)

In the base scenario ($8M revenue, $4M+ ARR, healthy margins), you become an attractive acquisition target for several categories of buyers. Vertical SaaS companies in adjacent trades (construction tech, industrial distribution software) would see you as an expansion play. Data and analytics companies that serve PE firms would see the PE network as the asset. Larger AI/data companies looking for vertical wedges could buy the platform.

Current SaaS valuation multiples for private companies: median is roughly 3-4x revenue, but vertical SaaS with strong NRR and PE-embedded customer relationships commands 5-8x. AI-native vertical SaaS with demonstrated traction is on the higher end of that range in the current market.

**Likely timing:** Year 4-6
**Likely valuation:** $25M-$65M (3-8x revenue depending on growth rate and ARR mix)
**Who buys you:** Vertical SaaS company (Procore, Brainshark, etc.), PE-focused data company, or a strategic like ServiceTitan/Watsco making a platform play

### Path 3: Growth-stage company / IPO path (Optimistic outcome)

In the optimistic scenario ($17M+ revenue, $11M ARR, multiple verticals, growing fast), you raise a Series A ($10-20M) and continue scaling. The company is on a path to $25-50M ARR within 2-3 more years, at which point you're either a Series B company or an attractive acquisition target for a much larger buyer at a much higher multiple.

Companies at $25M+ ARR in vertical SaaS with strong retention trade at 6-12x revenue in the current market. PE firms doing SaaS roll-ups are paying 4-6x for profitable companies with a path to 7-10x exit through growth. Public SaaS companies with NRR above 120% and mission-critical positioning have median multiples around 12x.

**Likely timing:** Year 5-8
**Likely valuation:** $100M-$300M+ (6-12x revenue at $25-50M ARR)
**Who buys you:** Large horizontal AI/data company (Palantir, Databricks, Snowflake), major PE-focused platform (Chronograph, Allvue), or you stay independent and raise growth rounds

### The exit that matters most for your planning

The base case — strategic acquisition in year 4-6 at $25-65M — is the most realistic outcome you should plan around. It's achievable without venture funding, it doesn't require everything to go right, and it produces a meaningful personal outcome. The optimistic case is worth reaching for but not worth betting your life on.

The bearish case is worth keeping in mind because even in the worst scenario, you've built a real business that generates cash, employs a small team, and could be sold for a modest amount. This is not a zero-or-hero bet. The downside is a decent small business, not a total loss.

**What's notable about this opportunity:** the downside is unusually good. Even the bearish case is a self-sustaining business doing $2.5M with a small team. Most startup ideas have a bimodal outcome — either it works big or you shut down. This one has a fat middle: even if the PE network effect is weaker than expected, even if sales cycles are longer than planned, you still end up with a real business selling real software to real customers who pay real money. The floor is high.

**What determines which scenario you land in:** three things, in order of importance. First, finding a co-founder (the difference between bearish and base). Second, landing the first customer fast (the difference between base and optimistic). Third, the strength of the PE network effect (determines how fast you compound after the first 3-5 customers). Everything else — technology, pricing, product — you can iterate on. Those three things you can't.

**The market is big enough.** Even at the narrow end (PE-backed HVAC distribution only), there's $9-18M of addressable year-one revenue. Add HVAC service rollups and you're at $50-100M. Add adjacent trades and you're at $150-300M. Add the self-serve long tail and you're over $500M. The expansion story is credible because the architecture is genuinely reusable across trades — you're not rebuilding from scratch each time. Whether you get to $500M depends on execution, but the market ceiling isn't the constraint.
